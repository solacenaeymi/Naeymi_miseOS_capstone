MiseOS: Kitchen Ordering & Inventory System

A recipe-driven ordering and inventory tool that helps specialty and fine-dining kitchens forecast ingredient needs accurately, cutting the guesswork out of over- and under-ordering.

Background

Why MiseOS

MiseOS is an iPad app that gives chefs and restaurant owners a fast, low-effort way to forecast ingredient quantities. It grew out of a simple observation from my years working in restaurants: the kitchens that ran smoothest weren't the ones with the most talented chefs, they were the ones where someone had reduced ordering to a system.

Small, specialty, and fine-dining restaurants source from local farms and specialty suppliers who offer limited quantities and little flexibility for last-minute changes. Most chefs are forecasting from memory, instinct, and a stack of spreadsheets; under real time pressure, with real financial consequences.

Problem

What Problem Was I Solving?

Specialty chefs need a fast, low-effort way to forecast ingredient quantities accurately, because getting this wrong either wastes food and money or leaves them short mid-service — but existing methods (manual math, spreadsheets, gut instinct) pull them away from the actual craft of cooking and don't scale well as recipes or order volume change.

Who

Restaurants: directly chefs and owners, and guests. Indirectly, front-of-house staff managing an 86'd dish.

What

Food waste, or not enough product: dishes get 86'd. Chefs often lack the math acumen, time, or energy to order precisely.

When

Depending on the restaurant, a daily or weekly issue: every ordering cycle carries the same risk.

Where

In restaurants, most acutely in small, specialty, and fine-dining kitchens sourcing from limited-flexibility suppliers.

Why It Happens

Chefs incorrectly account for the amount of food needed, over- or under-ordering, with no systematic check.

Why It Matters

Eliminating food waste and controlling overhead is critical as food costs keep rising for restaurants of every size.

Assumptions Vs. Unknowns Going In

Assumptions

  • Chefs tend to over-order to avoid running out, hoping to roll over ingredients with longer shelf life.
  • Restaurants would rather over-order slightly than under-order and 86 a dish.
  • Most chefs don't have the time or energy for in-depth inventory calculations.

Unknowns

  • How do chefs actually decide how much to order?
  • How much does over-ordering affect the bottom line?
  • Why haven't smaller restaurants adopted existing tools like Restaurant365?

Concept / Solution

The Proposed Solution

MiseOS turns a chef's own recipes into the source of truth for ordering: auto-calculating suggested orders from reservations, sales history, and recipe data, instead of asking chefs to do that math by hand.

Core User Stories

Suggested Ordering

As a chef, I want to see a suggested order automatically calculated from upcoming reservations and past service data, so I can place an accurate order in minutes rather than cross-referencing multiple records.

Recipe Scaling

As a chef, I want to input recipes for each menu item and scale them to my expected cover count, so my ingredient needs are calculated precisely and reflected automatically in my suggested order.

Kitchen-Matched Inventory

As a chef, I want to organize my inventory into sections that match my kitchen's physical layout, so I can walk through and assess stock quickly without switching screens or missing anything.

Cost Visibility

As a chef, I want to see my daily ordering spend compared to last week and last year, so I can catch cost creep early and adjust before it becomes a budget problem.

Budget Breakdown

As a restaurant owner, I want to see a weekly spend breakdown by ingredient category against my set budget, so I can identify where food costs are running high and make informed decisions.

Process

How I Got From Problem To Product

Ideation Research Testing Concept Evolution User Flows Visual Design Development

Ideation

Before landing on MiseOS, I explored several other directions rooted in hospitality: a shift and burnout companion for hospitality workers, and an artist-in-residence marketplace connecting hotels and restaurants with local artists. Revisiting old notebook pages, I found I'd sketched out ideas in this space even before the course began.

Once I landed on the restaurant ordering problem, ideation moved to a FigJam mindmap: an inventory app that integrates with POS to pull average order data, also pulling from reservation platforms like Resy, with recipes and dish components layered on top, and a strong emphasis on the result needing to be clear and easy to read at a glance.

Research

Competitive analysis. I looked at four platforms already operating in this space, to understand what was already solved well and where the gaps were for small and specialty operators:

Restaurant365 (Direct)

Restaurant inventory software tailored to large-scale chains and fast food, integrated with POS. Higher costs deter smaller businesses from adopting it.

MarketMan (Direct)

Simple design for inventory, with recipe management and in-house batch tracking (sauces, batched cocktails). A lot of detail can be captured per recipe.

MarginEdge (Direct)

Practical, user-friendly, built by restaurant operators with service kept front of mind. "Simple is key" was the standout takeaway, and a name none of my interviewees had heard of going in.

Toast POS (Indirect)

Built-in AI companion that can generate inventory from POS product data and accepts uploaded invoices, but as an added-cost module on top of a regular subscription, not a dedicated solution.

The throughline: dedicated platforms do their specific jobs well. The open question was how a new solution could seamlessly integrate into service alongside technology restaurant operators already use, rather than replacing it.

User interviews. I conducted six semi-structured interviews (three executive chefs and three restaurant owners/operators) to understand how ordering decisions actually get made day to day.

Ian A., Executive Chef

Built a custom order guide organized by kitchen workflow rather than ingredient type; uses Toast product-mix reports to estimate quantities per dish.

Julie L., Exec Chef / Culinary Director

Runs a manual, supplier-organized daily inventory system; wants pars standardized since prep quantities vary widely by staff member.

Matt P., Sous Chef / Head Baker

Keeps a manual Excel order guide updated daily; wants a simpler interface but is skeptical mistakes can ever be fully eliminated.

Bonnie W., GM / Bar Director

Juggles multiple disconnected vendor tools and logins; full cost visibility only arrives at month-end financials.

Dave F., Owner / Consultant

Has seen costly case-vs-unit ordering errors firsthand; believes any solution has to be "built for a labrador" to work for non-tech-savvy kitchens.

Martin T., Restaurant Owner

Reviews cost/waste reports weekly against budget; wants a hands-off role that still lets him sanity-check spend against industry benchmarks.

Synthesis. From open coding of these interviews, I built an affinity map of 15 thematic clusters: vendor relationships, the effects of 86'ing, food waste, unpredictability, rising food costs, human error, and more. Two patterns rose to the top: nearly every operator wanted an automated, POS-integrated ordering suggestion instead of "a glorified Excel," and any new system needed to be trainable in minutes given how little spare time kitchens have.

User Flows

Four core flows, walked through step by step: what triggers each one, what the system and the user each do, and what it resolves to.

Suggested Ordering

"As a chef, I want an auto-calculated suggested order so I can place an accurate order in minutes."

Trigger

Opens Suggested Orders Homepage — chef/manager starts an ordering session ahead of an upcoming service.

System · Step 1

Auto-calculates the order, pulling upcoming reservations, last week's same-day service, and last year's equivalent from POS and Resy.

Step 2

Reviews line items — sees each suggested quantity next to current on-hand stock.

Step 3

Flags low-confidence items where historical data is sparse, and uses their own judgment there.

Step 4

Adjusts quantities — edits any line item that doesn't match their read of the week.

Outcome

Confirms & places the order — it goes out in minutes instead of cross-referencing par-sheets, invoices, and reservations by hand.

Recipe Input & Scaling

"As a chef, I want to scale a recipe to my expected covers so prep and ordering both stay accurate."

Trigger

Opens a menu item — chef selects a dish (e.g. Roast Chicken) to build or edit its recipe.

Step 1

Inputs ingredients & per-cover amounts, building out each ingredient quantity for a single cover.

Step 2

Sets the planner to expected covers, seeing each suggested quantity next to current on-hand stock.

System · Step 3

Scales every ingredient, recalculating per-cover quantities instantly across the whole recipe.

System · Step 4

Links to Suggested Orders — ingredient quantities feed the ordering algorithm, grounded in real dish needs.

Outcome

Dish changes stay in sync — any future recipe edit automatically updates ordering logic, no separate update step.

Inventory Guide

"As a chef, I want my inventory organized like my kitchen so I can assess stock without jumping between screens."

Setup · One-Time

Defines their own sections — chef adds, renames, and reorders sections to mirror the kitchen's real layout.

Trigger

Opens the full inventory view, starting a stock check, often walking the physical space with the app in hand.

Step 1

Scans section by section — walks the walk-in, dry storage, etc. in the order the sections are laid out.

Step 2

Checks on-hand vs. par vs. suggested — each item shows all three numbers side by side, no separate lookup needed.

Step 3

Flags critical/low stock items, catching shortfalls in real time as they're seen on the shelf.

Outcome

Submits or exports the order without leaving the screen — one consolidated view from start to finish.

Financial Reporting

"As an owner, I want to compare daily spend to last week and last year so I can catch cost creep early."

Trigger

Opens Financial Reporting — chef or owner checks in on spend, typically mid-week or after a service.

System · Step 1

Shows daily spend in context: this week's daily order spend vs. the same day last week and last year.

Step 2

Reads the product-mix chart — a pie chart breaking spend down by category (proteins, produce, dairy).

Step 3

Checks the weekly budget tracker: total spend so far against the week's budget.

Step 4

Spots a trend or anomaly — e.g. proteins creeping up to 38% of spend, or a day that consistently runs over.

Outcome

Chef tightens up the category running over; owner confirms the kitchen is within budget or revisits targets.

Development

MiseOS was built and tested as a high-fidelity interactive prototype in Figma rather than a coded product. This capstone focused on research, interaction design, and validating the ordering logic end to end, not engineering implementation. The final V2 prototype includes full click-through flows for sign-in, recipe building and scaling, inventory walkthroughs, suggested ordering, and financial reporting.
